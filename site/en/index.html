A proposal for the Faculty of Education and Rehabilitation Sciences (ERF), University of Zagreb

One room, four uses

A room where the four walls are pictures and the ceiling is sound. In the morning it is a therapy room under the sea, later a movement analysis lab, and when it is quiet, an AI lab where whatever you describe is made by voice. Everything hangs from the ceiling except one subwoofer on the floor in a corner; otherwise the floor is for people only.

01 · Therapy

The room under the sea

A person in a wheelchair sits in the middle of the room, and a Caribbean reef swims around them. The sound comes from the whole room, as if they were really under the sea. Nothing has to be done. And when the person wants to, the room answers their movement and voice.

There is nothing on the floor except people and one subwoofer in a corner, away from where wheelchairs move. It weighs 27 kg and measures 527 x 462 x 363 mm, and a device that heavy is not hung above people. By each wall an ultra-short-throw laser projector hangs from the ceiling, and around the room there are eight small Genelec 8320A speakers on Genelec ceiling mounts. Beside each speaker is an Android phone: its screen shows what that speaker is playing, and its camera looks into the room and knows where the person is. When the person comes close to a speaker, its sound changes.

Movement game: relaxation and activation

My app Skeleton puts a skeleton of dots and lines over the picture of the person. The therapist chooses a movement, the app shows it as a transparent silhouette, and when the person hits it and holds it, a reward comes in picture and sound. The therapist sets the difficulty, from very gentle to more precise.

Relaxation

Slow, small movements: arms rise with the in-breath. Dark water and jellyfish. The reward is gentle: a whale sings from a corner of the room.

Activation

Bigger movements: arms out in a star, reach for a circle of light. A bright reef. The reward is cheerful: a school of fish sweeps through the whole room.

Sing and get your own song

The person sings a few bars into the microphone, the therapist presses a button, and in a minute a whole song made from that melody comes out of all the speakers. Suno ↗ (opens in a new tab) does this: a free account gives about ten songs a day for personal use. For the song to be sung in that person's own voice, a paid plan is needed and the person must confirm their voice themselves. For children, with the parents' consent.

Recording in 3D

In the middle of the ceiling hangs a Zylia, one ball with 19 microphones. It records the space in three dimensions, so a song, a circle of drumming or the therapist's voice can later be played back on the eight speakers exactly as it sounded. Four hanging microphones record from close up.

Colour, sound and music

My MilkDrop visualisations can also be on the walls: flowing pictures that come from music. For therapy I choose only slow presets, with no flashes.

myNoise: soundscapes

myNoise ↗ (opens in a new tab) is a website and an app with hundreds of soundscapes: rain, sea, forest, wind, fire, singing bowls. It was made by the Belgian sound engineer Stéphane Pigeon from his own recordings. People use it to relax, to sleep and to concentrate.

It is special because the sound is not a finished recording but ten channels that the listener adjusts, from deep rumble to tiny drops.

02 · Movement analysis

The movement lab: gait before and after therapy

The same space becomes a lab for analysing human movement, with no markers and no suits: it is enough to step into the circle.

A ring three metres across, with twelve phones all facing the centre, hangs from the ceiling. In the middle of the ring is a 3D microphone. The ring of phones films the person from all directions, and my app Skeleton marks the joints in every recording. From several cameras a three-dimensional skeleton is built, and it can be measured.

We record the same gait before and after therapy and compare it on the wall: step length, symmetry, rhythm, posture and the range of movement in the joints. The numbers do not replace the therapist's judgement; they give it a trace that can be followed over months.

Gait

Step length and rhythm, symmetry of the left and right side, before and after therapy.

Range of movement

How far an arm rises, a knee bends or the trunk turns, from session to session.

Balance

Body sway when standing and during transitions, with a game on the wall as motivation.

Slow motion

From twelve cameras a "bullet time" can also be made: a movement frozen and viewed from all sides, for teaching and for the person themselves.

For 3D joints from several cameras there is an open tool, Pose2Sim ↗ (opens in a new tab). Studies of such systems (e.g. OpenCap, 2023 ↗ (opens in a new tab)) show good accuracy for step rhythm and angles in the sagittal plane, and weaker accuracy for rotations and the foot, so the lab is a tool for tracking and research, not for diagnosis. The recordings stay on the computer in the room, with written consent.

03 · When the room is quiet

The AI lab: you speak, the room creates

The same walls, the same speakers, but without therapy: a workshop where an app, music, a film, a 3D world or a piece of research is created by voice.

When there is no therapy, I sit on the sofa, play quiet music and talk with Claude; what I describe appears on the walls. Music and a slow picture lead to a relaxed, alert state, the kind usually linked with alpha and theta rhythms; ideas come more easily to me in it. The hands stay free, with no keyboard and no mouse: only the voice and four walls.

  1. You sit on the sofa, the light dims, quiet music plays.
  2. You listen: the AI reads you a text or a script aloud, in a natural voice.
  3. You say what is unclear to you: "I don't understand this part, make me a storyboard."
  4. You watch a storyboard appear on the walls around you in a few minutes.
  5. You refine by voice: "Third shot closer, more light, add music." And again.

Claude is the brain of the room: it writes apps, arranges music in Ableton, edits in DaVinci Resolve, models and animates in Blender, and Higgsfield makes pictures, video and sound. Everything that is created is seen and heard at once in the whole room.

  • Claude Code the brain: understands, plans, writes
  • Ableton Live music and spatial sound
  • DaVinci Resolve editing and colour
  • Blender 3D and animation
  • Higgsfield pictures, video, sound in video
  • Speechify reading aloud

This is not the future, I already do it

I made all my apps by talking with Claude Code. In this room, things like that are made while sitting on the sofa.

Seven apps I have already made by voice
  • Blender Control: an AI on a Mac mini edits the open scene in Blender, takes screenshots and renders animations.
  • DaVinci Resolve Actions: buttons and scripts for colour correction, building timelines and exporting in DaVinci Resolve.
  • Ableton Live Actions: connects the mixing desk to Ableton and prepares the set by itself when it opens.
  • Vox to Txt: you press F1, speak, and the words are typed wherever the cursor is.
  • Mantra Manual Camera: a phone becomes a professional camera with manual settings and NDI output.
  • Cloud Mixer: mixes recordings, pictures and live streams into one programme with captions.
  • Shruti Box: drone, ragas and chords, and a voice trainer that guides you through your range.

04 · For the faculty

What the room gives ERF

Besides therapy, the room is a place for research, teaching, dissertations and experiments. Here is what I can imagine for your departments.

01

Movement research

Measurable data on gait and movement for the department's papers.

Example: the same group of children recorded at the start and at the end of a semester.

02

Doctoral dissertations

Recording, processing and showing data by voice, without programming.

Example: "Compare the step length for all twenty participants and draw a chart."

03

Case studies

Every session stays on record: movement, sound, the therapist's notes.

Example: an overview of one user's progress over six months, on the wall.

04

Comparing users

Charts that compare users, therapies or groups.

Example: two methods of therapy, side by side.

05

Teaching and practice

Students learn to watch movement and lead therapy with feedback.

Example: a slow-motion 3D recording of gait for a lecture.

06

Sketches and experiments

An idea spoken in the morning, an app or a scene tried in the afternoon.

Example: a new balance game for one girl.

07

Speech therapy

Recording voice and speech, and stories and pictures built around the words a child is practising.

Example: a story with five words with the sound R, with illustrations on the walls.

08

Autism and sensory needs

A calm room adapted to each child's sensory profile.

Example: quieter colours and fewer sounds for a child sensitive to stimuli.

09

Visual and hearing impairments

Orientation by sound from eight directions and a large, high-contrast display.

Example: a "where does the sound come from" game for a child with weaker sight.

Research on multisensory rooms is still scarce and the evidence is limited (e.g. a 2025 review ↗ (opens in a new tab)), so I see the room as a place that creates new research, not as a proven therapy. The therapist decides everything, and every recording is made with written consent.

05 · Project proposal

MIDIPan: games for the fingers

This is a proposal, not a finished app. I propose games that use a computer to practise finger dexterity.

The pictures are illustrations of the idea, made with artificial intelligence. They are not photographs of real people.

A device I have at home

MIDIPan ↗ (opens in a new tab) is a round touch-sensitive pad, like a handpan drum. It was made by Shiela Dixon (Etsy shop shieladixon). The pictures in the listing show twelve oval pads in a circle and one larger round pad in the middle. It sends MIDI over USB, so a computer can read it.

The idea

The person touches the middle and the game begins. A voice says, for example: “touch pad 1 with your index finger”, then with the ring finger, and so on through all ten fingers. The pads light up and show the way. For every correct touch a reward comes: on the projected wall of the room a character appears, for example a turtle. The computer leads, and the fingers loosen up.

I do not promise any result and I do not claim anything medical. This is an idea to develop together with people from the profession, for people with disabilities who want to practise fine motor skills through play.

I do not know what your faculty and your users actually need. Tell me what would help them, and we will adapt the game.

06 · Project proposal

OXI E16: controlling the room by hand

This too is a proposal, not a finished app. I propose a controller with sixteen knobs that changes the sound and light of the room by hand, without a screen.

The pictures are illustrations of the idea, made with artificial intelligence. They are not photographs of real people.

A controller I have

OXI E16 ↗ (opens in a new tab) is a MIDI controller from OXI Instruments. It has sixteen rotary knobs that turn smoothly, without clicks, and each is surrounded by a ring of coloured light. A small display shows the values. It is 114 mm wide, 143 mm deep, 36 mm high and weighs 0.37 kg, in an aluminium case. It connects to a computer by USB, and it also supports MIDI over a TRS connector and Bluetooth MIDI. Price 449 € (read on 22 November 2026, on the manufacturer's page).

The idea: one knob, one speaker

The room has eight speakers hanging around the person. Eight of the sixteen knobs would be one for each speaker. Every knob can also be pressed. Turning sets that speaker's volume, and pressing would step to the next sound on it. The ring of light shows by its colour which sound is on that speaker, and by its length how loud it is. The levels on the mixer can also be set from a phone with the CQ MixPad app; how the E16 knobs would change the mixer levels directly (the mixer receives MIDI over USB) still has to be worked out and tried.

So the person, or the therapist beside them, builds the sound around themselves: pressing browses the sounds, turning brings them up, and a different instrument can come from each direction. A sound does not have to be a recording; it can also be made (virtual). Whatever is turned is seen at once on the ring of the knob and on the wall.

The other eight knobs could, for example, change the colour and brightness of the light in the room, the speed of the projected picture and the main volume, plus a "calm" knob that quietly lowers everything together. Only slow changes, no flashing, as in the trial therapy mode.

The sound would come from our own local system, which I intend to develop for this room and which would run on the computer in the room, with its own sounds. It would work the way sound generators with separate sliders work; the best known is myNoise ↗ (opens in a new tab). Our system uses nothing of myNoise. None of this exists yet.

A first small set for this room

Chosen for a person who sits or lies, for low loudness, and for a therapist who can learn it quickly. Gongs and the didgeridoo are left for later: they are loud or need a strong breath.

  1. Two or three Himalayan metal bowls, medium size, with a soft mallet: a long, even tone; easy to play.
  2. One Koshi chime, for example Terra: quiet, hangs from a stand, needs no technique.
  3. An ocean drum (Remo or Meinl): the sea sound fits the reef; a very soft sound.
  4. A frame or buffalo drum, 16 inch (Remo), played softly: a slow pulse that a person can also join.
  5. A Hokema Sansula or kalimba: small and light; a person with limited reach can play one note at a time.
  6. A Stonewhistle clay flute (already on the page): chosen on the page because no combination sounds wrong.
  7. A children's lyre (Auris) or a small crystal bowl: to be decided with specialists; the crystal bowl is a higher, sharper tone.

07 · Project proposal

The wall that answers a touch

Can the projected wall react when a person touches it? Yes, in several ways. This is a proposal to explore with the faculty's specialists, not a finished product.

What exists

  • A depth camera hung from the ceiling sees a hand or body near the wall, and software turns that into a touch (Orbbec Femto Bolt ↗ (opens in a new tab) and Intel's RealSense cameras). It works at a distance and nothing is built into the wall. It sees a hand better than a fingertip.
  • The room's own cameras: eight phone cameras and the Skeleton app already find hands. The cheapest, but the least exact: it can say "a hand over the water", not "a touch at this millimetre".
  • A laser scanner along the wall (a "light curtain"; first shown at MIT as the LaserWall, today scanners from Hokuyo are used) sees a real fingertip touch, even on very large walls.
  • Infrared touch frames are made for flat screens, in the standard range up to about 55 inches. For a projected wall I could not confirm it.
  • An interactive projector with touch (Epson BrightLink 770Fi, area up to 100 inches; finger touch needs an extra unit). Simple, but it covers one screen, not a whole wall.
  • Ready-made systems for sensory and therapy rooms: LUMOplay (the maker estimates about 2,000 to 2,500 US dollars for a complete new system; read 22 November 2026), EyeClick (systems from 5,796 US dollars; read 22 November 2026) and omiWall (price on request).

A touch becomes a mouse or touch event, or travels as TUIO, an open protocol that game engines and tools like Unity can read. The room's Mac mini can then react.

What fits this room

I would try a depth camera hung from the ceiling beside the projector first. It fits the rule that nothing is on the walls, and on the floor only the subwoofer in its corner. A laser scanner low along the wall sees a real touch, but it would stand at the foot of the wall and break that rule, so it comes only as a second step, if finer touch is wanted.

A cheap first step: one camera and the trial period of ready-made software on one wall. On site it has to be checked how high and how far from the wall a seated person's hand is, whether the wall is flat, and how the light of the other projectors reaches the camera.

What could be explored with the faculty's specialists

  • Touch the water and it ripples: slow, calm, a cause and an effect.
  • A fish follows the hand, and the person guides it across the wall.
  • Touch a glowing shape to say "done".
  • Targets at different heights: high for arms, low for hands, small for fingers.
  • Two people touching together, so something happens only when both touch.
  • Everything slow, no flashing, and a miss is never punished. The therapist sets the size of the targets and how long they stay.

Limits: a camera sees a hand near the wall and does not feel a real touch, so targets must be big and forgiving. With front projection a person standing at the wall casts a shadow on the picture, as the pictures show. For fine finger work MIDIPan in section 05 is the better tool. I promise no result and claim nothing medical.

08 · Project proposal

Figures of light in the room

A free-floating hologram in the air does not exist as a product; "hologram" is a trade name for tricks that make a figure look as if it stands in the room. So here I speak of a figure made of light. This is a proposal to explore with the faculty's specialists.

What fits this room

Three levels, from simple to special:

  1. A character on the wall that turns to the person and reacts. A turtle, a fish or a guide drawn in light, on the projected reef. It needs no new hardware, only the room's software and cameras. It comes closer, looks around and goes away when the therapist says.
  2. A figure on gauze in one corner. A thin see-through cloth hung from the ceiling in front of the wall, with a projector shining on it, can make an animal or a guide look as if it stands in the room. It has to be tested: this room's projector may not suit it, and light from the other walls would spoil it. A hand passes through cloth, so it must be fixed so nobody gets tangled.
  3. A transparent screen. Expensive for a small figure, and a glass object in a room where wheelchairs move.

What does not fit: spinning LED fans (sellers warn about the spinning blades and advise hanging them out of reach), volumetric displays (too small) and hologram boxes (cabinets as big as a person). Headsets close the room off and are heavy, which some may find distressing; age limits and fit I did not research.

A cheap first step: one animal character on one wall, reacting to a hand through the cameras the room already has. If the faculty likes it, try a gauze in one corner.

All the approaches in one overview
  • Pepper's ghost with a foil: the figure looks as if it stands behind the foil; the hidden space must be dark.
  • Gauze with projection: a large figure, a hand passes through but the effect breaks; dim light.
  • Transparent OLED screen: LG 55 inch, about 38% transparency; a glass screen.
  • Hologram box: Proto Luma, 88 by 50 by 24 inches; a cabinet.
  • Spinning LED fan: HYPERVSN; blades spin at high speed.
  • Light-field display: Looking Glass, up to 27 inches for a group, a viewing cone of 53 degrees, no glasses.
  • Volumetric display: Voxon VX1, 18 by 18 by 8 cm; moving parts inside.
  • Fog screen: FogScreen; you can walk through it; check moisture and breathing with specialists.
  • Headset: only the wearer sees it; Microsoft ended HoloLens 2 in 2024.

What a guide could do

These are ideas to explore with specialists, not a claim of effect.

  • Show a movement to copy: a guide of light slowly raises an arm, and the person copies it when ready.
  • Wait: the guide stands still and does not hurry or score.
  • Nod when it is done, with a gentle light.
  • An animal that comes closer when the person is calm or reaches out.
  • Always under the therapist's control, with a button that switches the figure off at once. Some people may find a figure unsettling, so it is optional and can be a simple shape instead of a face.

Connection diagram

Everything is run by one Mac mini M6 with 24 GB of memory. The Mac drives three projectors, and the fourth goes through a DisplayLink adapter. The phones send their picture over Wi-Fi.

The path of the sound

The sound goes through an analogue chain. The Mac mini sends it to the Allen & Heath CQ-20B mixer over the mixer's USB interface, and microphones and instruments go to the mixer's inputs. The mixer has eight balanced XLR outputs. Seven of them go into the inputs of the Genelec 7360A subwoofer, which takes the bass below 85 Hz from them, and through its outputs the signals go on, without the bass, to seven Genelec 8320A speakers on the ceiling. The eighth speaker gets its signal straight from the mixer, so it plays the full range down to its own 55 Hz (−6 dB). I imagine it as the speaker above the door; the choice is confirmed by measuring.

The 85 Hz crossover is fixed when working without a computer; with Genelec's GLM set it can be set between 50 and 100 Hz. The 8320A speakers have no switches: the input level and other settings are made in GLM, which is why the GLM set is in the table. The cables run along the ceiling: about 1 m from the mixer to the subwoofer and about 10 m from the subwoofer to each speaker. That is an estimate, because the places of the subwoofer and the mixer decide the lengths. The mixer has no ninth output, but its Alt Out can duplicate the eighth speaker's output and send it to the subwoofer's LFE input, so that its bass also reaches the subwoofer; that has to be tried. The mixer can also be controlled from a phone with the CQ MixPad app over the mixer's own Wi-Fi.

Android or iPhone?

I recommend Android. On Android I develop apps myself and install them without a store, and the Mac controls them over USB or Wi-Fi. Skeleton already receives the picture from several Android phones at once. An iPhone has an excellent camera, but it costs more, apps go only through Apple's store, and the Mac can use it as a camera only one at a time.
